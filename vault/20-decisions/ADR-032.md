## Context

PRD §44A.39 lists two acceptance criteria that pull in opposite directions
until you look closely:

- "every movement has a structural anchor and reason codes";
- "policy may conclude that leaving the original stop unchanged is optimal for
  a position".

The obvious implementation returns `None` when nothing should move. That
satisfies the second and quietly defeats the first, because the interesting
question about a stop that did not move is *why* — and there are at least six
answers: no anchor qualified, the improvement was under the threshold, a
cooldown was active, the data was frozen, the anchor was in the future, or
moving would have widened risk.

Downstream, all six look identical: the stop stayed where it was. In a
counterfactual replay they are the difference between a policy that is being
careful and one that is broken.

## Decision

**`propose` always returns a `StopProposal`.** A hold is a proposal whose price
equals the current stop, carrying the same anchor reference (where one existed)
and at least one reason code naming why it held.

There is no `None` return and no separate hold type.

## Consequences

- **The replay can count reasons.** "This policy held 400 times, 380 of them
  for cooldown" is a finding about the configuration; "the policy held 400
  times" is not.
- **The two acceptance criteria stop competing.** Every proposal has a reason,
  and one of the reasons is that holding was right.
- **A caller cannot accidentally treat a hold as an absence.** `if proposal:`
  on an optional return is the bug this shape removes — it would silently skip
  the recording of a deliberate decision.
- **The reason codes are an enumeration, not free text**, so the replay can
  aggregate them and a test can assert on them without matching strings.
